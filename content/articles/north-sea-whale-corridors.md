The North Sea is one of the most intensively used marine areas on Earth: shipping, fishing, oil and gas platforms, and increasingly, offshore wind farms. Straight through it travel harbour porpoises, minke whales and occasionally even humpbacks — along routes that until recently were largely unknown.

## Listening Instead of Searching

A network of anchored hydrophones records the clicks and songs of marine mammals around the clock. Combined with satellite tags, a movement picture is emerging for the first time: preferred migration corridors, seasonal hotspots, avoidance behaviour when noise levels rise.

## Noise Is the Invisible Barrier

Pile-driving for wind turbine foundations, seismic surveys and ship propellers generate sound levels that whales avoid — or that can injure them outright.

> "For an animal that sees with its ears, a loud sea is a dark sea," explains one marine biologist.

## Planning With the Data

The new maps are already changing practice:

- **Construction windows** are scheduled to avoid peak migration periods.
- **Bubble curtains** dampen pile-driving noise many times over.
- **Temporary speed limits** for ships reduce collision risk in hotspot zones.

The North Sea will remain industrial. But for the first time, the routes of its largest residents are systematically flowing into the planning process — a quiet but real step forward.
