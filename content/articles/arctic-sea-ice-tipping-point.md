To understand the Arctic, you need to distinguish between two kinds of ice: first-year ice, which forms each winter and melts each summer, and multi-year ice, which survives for decades and can grow several metres thick. That old ice is the backbone of the polar cap — and it is disappearing.

## An Ocean Changing Colour

Sea ice acts like a vast mirror, reflecting up to 80 percent of incoming sunlight back into space. Open water absorbs most of that energy instead. Every square kilometre stripped of ice warms the ocean a little more — accelerating the melt of whatever ice remains.

> "We are no longer watching a linear decline. We are watching a system that feeds on itself," says a polar researcher who has measured ice thickness for two decades.

## What Is at Stake

The consequences reach far beyond the Arctic circle:

- **Weather extremes:** A warmer north weakens the jet stream, favouring stalled weather patterns across Europe.
- **Sea levels:** Melting sea ice does not raise oceans directly, but it accelerates the retreat of Greenland's land-based glaciers, which do.
- **Ecosystems:** From ice algae to polar bears, an entire food web is anchored to the frozen surface.

## A Closing Window

Climate models are clear on one point: whether the Arctic becomes reliably ice-free in summer depends largely on emissions over the next two decades. The difference between 1.5 and 2 degrees of global warming will decide whether multi-year ice has a future — or becomes a footnote in climate history.
