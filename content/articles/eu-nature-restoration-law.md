Conservation law was defensive for a long time: it prohibited making degraded conditions worse. The restoration regulation flips that logic — it obliges member states to actively repair damaged ecosystems, with measurable targets and fixed deadlines.

## The Core Obligations

- **Area targets:** A growing share of degraded land and marine ecosystems must be placed under restoration — first by 2030, then in stages beyond.
- **Peatlands:** Drained agricultural peat soils — enormous CO₂ sources — are to be progressively rewetted.
- **Rivers:** Thousands of kilometres are to flow freely again through the removal of obsolete barriers.
- **Cities:** No net loss of urban green space; tree canopy cover is to grow.
- **Pollinators:** The decline of insect populations is to be reversed.

## The Fight Behind It

Few environmental laws have been more contested. Farming associations feared losing productive land; the final text contains flexibility clauses and funding commitments.

> "The law is a compromise — but it is the first time restoration becomes enforceable," says one environmental lawyer.

## How Success Will Be Measured

The national restoration plans are decisive: they translate EU targets into concrete areas, budgets and responsibilities. Paper does not rewet a peatland — but it makes inaction justiciable for the first time.
