  - **Dänemark:** Über die Energieinsel Bornholm sollen künftig Windparks mit mehreren Gigawatt an Dänemark und Deutschland angeschlossen werden. Die Angaben zur Größe schwanken zwischen 2 und 3 Gigawatt.[^6]

  ## Was bremst

  Zwischen einer politischen Zusage und Strom im Netz liegen Jahre. Engpässe sind Netzanschlüsse und Seekabel, spezialisierte Installationsschiffe, Häfen mit Schwerlastkais und Fachkräfte. Hinzu kommen gestiegene Kosten und Ausschreibungen, die für Investoren nicht attraktiv genug sind. Das zeigt das Beispiel Litauen.

  ## Die Chance: Netze über Grenzen

  Der interessanteste Trend ist grenzüberschreitend: Hybride Projekte verbinden einen Windpark zugleich mit zwei Ländern und dienen als Stromtrasse zwischen ihnen. For wind planners, the Baltic Sea is favourable territory: shallow water, reliable wind, short distances to shore and almost no tides. In August 2022, a few months after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, the countries around it set themselves an ambitious goal.

## The Marienborg Pledge

In the **Marienborg Declaration** of 30 August 2022, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Germany, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland and Sweden agreed to expand Baltic offshore wind to **at least 19.6 gigawatts** by 2030. That would be seven times the 2.8 gigawatts installed at the time.[^1] In the long run, the countries see potential for up to 93 gigawatts by 2050.[^1]

## Where the Build-Out Stands

By 2023, capacity in the region had grown to only about **3.1 gigawatts**.[^2] Since then, things have started to move:

- **Poland:** The **Baltic Power** wind farm first delivered electricity to the Polish grid on 10 July 2026.[^3] By early October, all 76 turbines were installed and the project was being commissioned. Its capacity is given as 1.14 to 1.2 gigawatts, depending on the source.[^4] It is Poland's first offshore wind farm.
- **Lithuania:** The second tender for 700 megawatts failed because only one bidder, the utility Ignitis, took part.[^5] As of September 2026, the site still had no developer.[^5]
- **Denmark:** The Bornholm energy island is meant to connect several gigawatts of wind farms to Denmark and Germany. Stated figures for its size vary between 2 and 3 gigawatts.[^6]

The countries have not published an up-to-date total for the Baltic. Across Europe, the industry association WindEurope now expects only about 70 of the targeted 120 gigawatts of offshore wind to be built by 2030.[^7] Unless the pace picks up significantly, the Baltic's 19.6-gigawatt target is likely to be missed as well.

## What Is Holding It Back

Years lie between a political pledge and electricity in the grid. The bottlenecks are grid connections and subsea cables, specialised installation vessels, ports with heavy-load quays, and skilled workers. On top of that come higher costs and tenders that are not attractive enough for investors, as Lithuania shows.

## The Opportunity: Grids Across Borders

The most interesting trend is cross-border: hybrid projects connect a wind farm to two countries at once and double as a power link between them. An early example is the connection via Denmark's Kriegers Flak and Germany's Baltic 2 wind farms, which has been able to carry electricity between the two countries since 2020. Bornholm is meant to apply the same principle on a larger scale.

If the build-out succeeds, the Baltic will move from sideshow to one of Europe's important power plants, and to a piece of independence from imported gas.
